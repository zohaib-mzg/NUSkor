Ask a hundred students for their worst academic memory and a startling number will not mention an exam. They will mention the group project: the member who vanished, the final week merge catastrophe, the argument that surfaced precisely never, until the presentation. Group coursework fails for a specific reason — it is a coordination problem wearing a coursework costume — and coordination problems have known solutions that are mostly boring, mostly social, and reliably effective. Here is the whole system, from day one to the quiet member.

## Day one: define “done” before defining who does what

The opening meeting should produce a one-page scope document, not a pep talk. It needs four things:

- **The deliverables list** — every artifact you must hand in, exactly as the brief names them.
- **A definition of done** per deliverable — what “finished” means. “The report is done when it compiles figures and results consistent with the final code version” prevents an entire class of deadline disputes.
- **The real deadline, minus two days.** The internal deadline is the deadline; the official one is a safety net for the person pulling everything together.
- **The scope fence.** Explicitly list what you are *not* building. Scope creep is the leading killer of group projects, and it always arrives by someone saying “wouldn't it be cool if”, in week three, about the least critical feature.

Ten minutes of written clarity on day one saves roughly one week of meetings later. Take the minutes, share them in the group channel, and get a quick “confirmed” from everyone. The point is not bureaucracy; it is a shared, checkable source of truth.

## Split by deliverable, not by vibe

With the deliverables defined, assign **one owner per deliverable**. Shared ownership means no ownership, everywhere, always. A useful split for a software project: one person owns the data layer, one the interface, one the tests and packaging, one the report skeleton — with everyone contributing to overlapping parts as the schedule allows.

Two rules make the split work:

- **Interfaces first.** Before anyone goes deep, agree on the contract between parts: the data format, the function signatures, the report section headings. A two-page interface agreement lets four people work in parallel without colliding. Without it, four people build four things that cannot be joined.
- **Owners can ask, cannot reassign quietly.** If someone is stuck, the first move is asking for help, not silently sliding their deliverable elsewhere. Silent sliding is how resentments and duplicate work both get created.

## The cadence that prevents surprises

A fifteen-minute weekly check — same slot, once a week — is enough for most projects. Standing agenda: each owner says what moved, what is next, what is blocked. Blockers get an owner and a date, or the date gets escalated. Keep decisions in one place, not scattered across chat history and three people's memories.

At the halfway mark, run a twenty-minute honest review: is the scope still realistic, is the integration path still alive, does anyone need to hand something over? A project that is behind schedule in the middle can almost always be rescued by cutting scope; the same project discovered behind in the final week usually cannot.

## When a member goes quiet

Escalate in order, early, factually:

1. **A private message** — “missing you this week, everything okay?” People sometimes have real lives happening; assume that first.
2. **The group, kindly:** “we noticed X hasn't moved; can we reallocate and keep you informed?” Not an ambush; a logistics update.
3. **The instructor or teaching assistant**, with documentation — dates, deliverables, attempts to make contact. This is why the paper trail from day one exists. Instructors see this regularly and almost always prefer hearing about it in week nine rather than week fifteen.

Throughout: keep a simple contribution log — who owned what, when it was due, whether it landed. Not for war; for clarity.

## FAQ

### Friends or skills — who should be in the group?

Both ratios matter, but the underrated trait is reliability. Someone with average skill who replies and delivers is worth more than a star who is never reachable. If you must choose, and you can, use a small first task — a ten-minute contribution in week one — as a sample of the person's follow-through.

### A member contributed nothing and there is peer assessment. What now?

Answer honestly in the assessment instrument and back it with your contribution log; these systems exist precisely for that situation. Send the quiet member a heads-up that you are doing so, in writing — not as a threat, as manners. Most peer systems also let you explain nuance, so use the comment field for facts, not adjectives.

### How many meetings does a group project actually need?

Fewer, but fixed: one kickoff, one weekly fifteen-minute check, one final integration review. Everything else is asynchronous. Meetings feel like work but they are rarely the bottleneck; unowned deliverables and unagreed interfaces are.

## The takeaway

Group projects reward coordination hygiene, not heroics: scope written on day one, one owner per deliverable, interfaces agreed before parallel work, a weekly fifteen-minute rhythm, and an escalation ladder that starts friendly and ends documented.
