The paper comes back with a number far below the one you pictured, and for about three days it feels like the semester is over. It usually is not — and the proof is arithmetic, not encouragement. Midterms rarely carry enough weight to end a course on their own. What ends courses is the response: either spiralling, or a factual 48-hour recovery plan. This post is the plan.

## First 48 hours: get the facts

Feelings are fast and unreliable; receipts are slow and useful. Before deciding anything, collect three facts.

**The weightage.** What share of the course was the midterm? A 25% midterm at a poor score is a problem; a 10% quiz you are calling a midterm is not.

**The damage, computed.** Suppose the midterm was 25% of the course and you scored 40%. You banked 0.40 × 25 = 10 points out of 25 — a deficit of up to 15 points against a perfect run. The remaining 75% of the course is still live. If the rest of your graded work sits around 70%, your course is still mathematically recoverable to a respectable total. Run your own numbers with the [course total calculator](/tools/course-total-calculator); watching the arithmetic be smaller than the feeling is the first constructive step.

**The breakdown.** Which parts of the paper cost you the marks — one section, one skill, or everything? A single catastrophic section suggests a preparation gap; uniform mediocrity suggests a study-method problem. The diagnosis decides the fix.

## Diagnose honestly

Most bad midterms trace to one of four causes, and they have different treatments:

- **Content gap.** You did not know the material. Treatment: rebuild understanding from first principles — worked examples first, re-derivation second, past papers third.
- **Exam skill gap.** You knew it but could not produce it under time pressure. Treatment: timed practice. The skill gap is invisible until the clock is running, which is why it ambushes good students.
- **Time or load problem.** Other courses crowded it out. Treatment: a weekly plan with per-course hours, not intentions.
- **Signal problem.** You studied the wrong emphasis. Treatment: questions in office hours, past-paper patterns, and paying attention to what the instructor keeps returning to.

## Rebuild the plan

With causes identified, the rebuild is specific:

1. **Recompute what the remaining weight demands.** The [final grade calculator](/tools/grade-calculator) answers it directly: given your current score, the graded weight so far, and your target, here is the score you need on what remains. If it is impossible, the tool will say so and show your realistic ceiling — better to know in week eight than week fifteen.
2. **Fix the largest cause first.** The four-cause list is roughly ordered by effort-to-effect. Content gaps take the most work; signal problems the least.
3. **Talk to the course staff.** Not to negotiate the mark — to calibrate. Asking “what distinguished the strong answers?” gets actionable information most students never collect.
4. **Protect your other courses.** A recovery plan that sacrifices two other courses to save one is not a recovery plan; it is a transfer of damage. The improvements planner for your CGPA can show how much each course is actually worth in the total.

One note on sequencing: do the arithmetic before the emotional processing, every time. A deficit that looks catastrophic becomes either a solvable target or an honest ceiling the moment it is computed — and both of those are easier to carry than an unknown. If the remaining weight genuinely cannot close the gap, zoom out to the level of the whole degree with the [CGPA improvement plan](/blog/how-to-raise-your-cgpa): a course ceiling is rarely a semester ceiling.

## The psychology, briefly

One number is not a character assessment, and the semester is not a trial by midterm. What midterms are excellent at is producing a clear signal early enough to act on it — which is precisely why they exist. Treat yours as old information the moment the plan is made; the plan, not the paper, is now the interesting document.

## FAQ

### I failed the midterm badly. Is the course lost?

Usually not on weightage alone — a 25% midterm caps at a 75-point loss only if the rest is also weak. Run the numbers: many “failed” midterms still allow a pass or better with strong finals. The [final grade calculator](/tools/grade-calculator) tells you the required final score for any target.

### Should I drop or withdraw from the course?

That is a policy question with real consequences, and the rules (deadlines, transcript effects) live with your institution, not here. Get the facts from your academic office first, then decide with real information rather than midterm-week emotion.

### How do I stop replaying the bad result?

Give it a deadline. Let the 48-hour window be for feelings plus facts, and end it with the written plan. Discipline works better than suppression: once you have a next action, the loop has somewhere to go.

## The takeaway

A bad midterm is one weighted component, and most components are smaller than they feel. Collect the facts, name the cause, recompute the required score, and let the remaining weightage do what it was always going to do — decide the course.
