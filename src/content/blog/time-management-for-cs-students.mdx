Every CS semester has a week where the project demo, the problem set, the quiz and the lab report are all due within about seventy-two hours of each other. In that week, students who rely on motivation get a lesson about its exchange rate. Students who rely on a system — a small, boring, five-part system — mostly notice that the week was busy and move on. This post describes the system at that size: nothing that requires a lifestyle overhaul, just enough structure that deadlines stop being surprises.

## Part one: the semester map

Before any weekly system, spend one focused hour building the semester map: every course, every graded component, its weight and its due date, in one place. Paper, a notes file, a spreadsheet — the medium does not matter; the consolidation does.

The map pays off three ways. Deadlines stop hiding in six different course outlines. Clusters become visible — the map is usually how you first notice that weeks 8 and 14 are structurally awful, which is information available in week 1 and actionable from about week 6. And weights become comparable: with every [course total](/tools/course-total-calculator) sitting in the same view, “how much does this assignment actually cost me?” stops being a guess.

## Part two: the weekly review

Once a week, in a fixed slot you choose — Sunday evening and Friday afternoon are both popular, the specific slot matters less than its fixedness — spend thirty minutes doing four things:

1. **Look back** at what was graded since the last review. Update each course's running total where results landed.
2. **Look forward** seven days: what is due, what is being assessed, which classes have announced quizzes.
3. **Pick three priorities** for the week — not fifteen, three. If everything is a priority, the list has failed.
4. **Check the next cluster** on the semester map and start the longest-lead item early, before it joins a pile.

This half hour is the highest-leverage time in the system: it converts deadlines from ambushes into entries on a list. Skip it for three weeks and the whole structure quietly dissolves, which is why the slot has to be protected like a class.

## Part three: time-boxing without fiction

Time-boxing fails when people plan the week they wish they had. Plan the week you actually have:

- **Blocks of 60 to 90 minutes** for deep work — a problem set, a project feature, a lab. Shorter boxes for review and admin.
- **Anchor blocks to existing structure.** “After the Tuesday lecture, two hours in the library” beats “find time sometime”. Existing commitments do the remembering for you.
- **Buffer, deliberately.** Never book 100% of available hours; book about 80%. The remaining fifth absorbs the estimate that was wrong, the laptop that needed updating, and the group meeting that ran long. A plan with no slack is a plan that is always broken.
- **One deliverable per box.** “Work on project” is not a deliverable; “implement the login flow end to end” is. Vague boxes convert into guilt; concrete ones convert into done.

## Part four: when everything is due at once

Triage rules for the collision week, in order:

- **Rank by weight × days remaining**, not by which deadline shouts loudest. A 20% deliverable due Thursday outranks a 3% quiz due Wednesday, even though Wednesday comes first.
- **Decide who gets the B-plus.** Something gets less than full effort in a collision week; that is arithmetic, not failure. Choose the victim in advance — usually the lowest weight — rather than letting panic choose randomly.
- **Ask early, not late.** Extension queries, clarifications, teammates’ blockers — all of these get better answers four days out than four hours out.
- **Ship the smallest complete version first.** A working core submitted early beats a polished everything submitted at the deadline, and it is not close when submissions fail at 23:58.

## Part five: protect the inputs

The system runs on a person, and the person runs on sleep. Two fixed sleep and meal windows are worth more to your recall than two extra hours of midnight cramming — memory consolidates overnight, and a tired brain reads slower, decides worse and quits earlier. The practical version is unglamorous: a consistent sleep window, meals away from the desk occasionally, and the recognition that a planned rest block is part of the system, not a betrayal of it.

## FAQ

### Every week looks different. How do I plan at all?

Anchor everything that can be anchored: a fixed weekly review, fixed blocks around your timetable, fixed review sessions. The variable parts slot into the anchored skeleton instead of replacing it. Plans built from anchors survive chaos; plans built from wake-up times do not.

### I never follow my time-blocks exactly. Are they still worth it?

Yes. A plan's first job is not to be obeyed but to make conflicts visible: when two deadlines cannot both fit, you find out on Sunday rather than in the panic. Even a sixty-percent-followed plan that surfaces impossible weeks in advance beats no plan plus adrenaline.

### How does this interact with group work?

Group projects add other people's calendars to your collision weeks, and the same triage logic applies at team level: one owner per deliverable, an explicit internal deadline before the real one, and a shared status check that costs minutes not meetings. The [group project guide](/blog/group-projects-without-the-drama) covers the coordination layer in detail.

## The takeaway

Time management is not a personality trait; it is a small system: a semester map, a protected weekly review, honest 80% time-boxing, a triage rule for collision weeks, and inputs worth running on. Build the map this week, keep the review sacred, and the worst weeks of the semester become merely busy.
