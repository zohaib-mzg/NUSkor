Attendance is the quietest component in every course. It rarely makes a dramatic midterms-week headline, but it moves grades through three separate channels: participation marks where they exist, content you cannot reconstruct from slides alone, and — in many institutions — a minimum threshold that can block you from sitting the final at all. This post is not a lecture about showing up. It is the arithmetic of showing up, so you can answer the only question that matters: how much room do I actually have left?

## Know your policy before doing any math

Three different things can be true at once in the same institution, so read the actual rules for each course:

- **A stated minimum.** Many courses require a percentage of classes attended — commonly in the region of 75%, but the number and its strictness are entirely local. Some institutions set one university-wide figure; others let courses set their own.
- **A consequence.** The penalty for crossing the line varies: a marks deduction, a warning stage, mandated extra work, or exclusion from the final exam. The difference between these outcomes is enormous, and it lives in your handbook, not in a friend's summary of it.
- **A participation component.** Separately from any minimum, attendance and in-class engagement frequently form part of the sessional or class-work marks — a straight grade contribution with none of the drama.

Spend ten minutes with the course outline and handbook once; the rest of this post assumes you know which regime you are in.

## The arithmetic of absences

The core quantity is simple: **attendance percentage = classes attended ÷ classes held so far**. Everything else is a variation. Two questions follow from it.

**How many can I still miss?** Suppose the minimum is 75% and 40 classes have been held, of which you attended 34. Missing more is constrained by the total, including future classes. If 10 classes remain, the semester holds 50 in total, and the minimum means you must attend at least 0.75 × 50 = 37.5, so 38 classes. You have 34 — every remaining class is required, and you would finish at 38 ÷ 50 = 76%. Tight, but safe.

**Am I already below the line?** Reuse the same numbers from the other direction. If you attended only 28 of the first 40 classes (70%), reaching the 75% minimum by the end requires attending at least 9.5 — all 10 — of the remaining classes, finishing at 76%. The math does not negotiate; it just tells you the schedule. The [attendance calculator](/tools/attendance-calculator) runs both directions — “what can I miss?” and “what must I attend?” — for whatever minimum your course actually uses.

Notice the asymmetry that makes attendance math sneaky: percentages get harder to fix over time, because each new class adds to both the numerator and the denominator, and the deficit you are fighting grows with every class held. An absence in week 3 is cheaper to absorb than the same absence in week 13. Nothing about the class itself changes; only the buffer does.

## If you must miss a class

Sometimes the absence is unavoidable — so make it as recoverable as possible:

- **Choose the most recoverable absence.** A lecture with recorded video or dense textbook coverage costs less than a lab you can never redo or a discussion section whose value is the discussion.
- **Borrow structure, not just notes.** Someone's screenshot of the board is less useful than five minutes explaining what the instructor emphasised.
- **Check what was announced.** Deadlines and scope changes are announced live; reconstruct the announcements, not just the content.
- **Do the work anyway.** The assignment does not care whether you were there for the briefing.

## Building an attendance rhythm

The students who never think about attendance math are usually the ones who made attendance their default: they schedule other commitments around classes rather than deciding each morning; they treat the first five minutes — announcements, scope hints, example patterns — as the highest-value part of the lecture; they batch errands into the same slots each week. It is not discipline in the heroic sense. It is just a system with fewer daily decisions in it.

## FAQ

### Can attendance actually affect my grade?

In many courses, yes — sometimes through a participation or sessional component, sometimes through minimum thresholds, sometimes both. The honest answer is course-specific, and the outline is where you check.

### I'm already below the minimum. Now what?

First, confirm the exact rule and its consequence — some systems have warning stages before any penalty, and some have none. Second, talk to the course instructor or teaching assistant early rather than at the end; options that exist in week 8 sometimes evaporate by week 15. Third, compute the recovery path: it is usually “attend everything remaining”, and knowing that precisely beats fearing it vaguely.

### Do medical or emergency absences count?

Policies vary widely, including documentation requirements and whether special consideration replaces the absence or merely excuses it. That is a question for your institution's designated office, and it is worth asking before the situation becomes urgent. ## The takeaway

Attendance is arithmetic with a deadline: attended classes over classes held, checked against a minimum you should verify locally. Calculate your runway early, protect the final stretch, and when something must give, make it the most recoverable class rather than the largest lecture.
